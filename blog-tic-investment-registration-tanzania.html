TIC Investment Registration Explained: Who Qualifies and What It Unlocks

A clear breakdown of who qualifies for a TIC Certificate of Incentives, what it costs, and the tax, land, and legal benefits it unlocks.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

If you have been growing a business in Tanzania and someone recently told you to "get your TIC certificate," you are not alone in wondering what that actually means in practice. TIC investment registration, now administered under the Tanzania Investment and Special Economic Zones Authority following the 2025 restructuring, is not a second layer of company registration. It is something entirely different, a certificate that unlocks real financial and practical benefits, but only for investors who meet specific conditions. Let us walk through exactly who qualifies and what you stand to gain once that certificate lands in your hands.

1. What a TIC Certificate of Incentives actually is

Once your company already exists through BRELA, the Certificate of Incentives is a separate document that recognises your project as a qualifying investment under the Tanzania Investment Act. It does not replace your certificate of incorporation, it sits alongside it, unlocking a package of tax relief, easier access to land and work permits, and legal protections that ordinary registered companies do not automatically receive. Think of BRELA as proving your business exists, and this certificate as proving your investment is significant enough to earn special treatment from the government.

2. The capital thresholds that decide who qualifies

This is where most confusion clears up quickly. Qualification is not based on your industry or your ambition, it comes down to a specific capital figure. A project wholly owned by Tanzanian citizens currently needs a minimum investment of around fifty thousand US dollars. A project that is foreign owned, or a joint venture involving foreign shareholders, needs to show at least five hundred thousand US dollars. There is also a higher tier for what the authority calls strategic investments, generally around fifty million US dollars, which unlocks an even broader set of incentives reserved for the largest projects entering the country.

3. What you need in hand before you even apply

You cannot walk into this process empty handed. Before applying, you need a certified copy of your certificate of incorporation, your memorandum and articles of association, and proof that the capital you are claiming actually exists, usually shown through a stamped bank statement covering several months of activity or evidence of a substantial share of the investment already committed. You will also need proof of access to land for your project, whether through ownership, a lease, or an allocation letter, along with a board resolution formally authorising the registration.

Wellbiz tip: Start gathering your certified incorporation documents, stamped bank statements, and land proof as soon as your project approaches the qualifying capital threshold — a complete application package is what separates a few-week approval from a months-long back-and-forth.

4. The document checklist that gets your application moving

Beyond the basics, the authority expects a properly written business plan or feasibility study that lays out your capital expenditure, expected cash flows, and how many local jobs your project will create. This is not a box ticking exercise, reviewers actually read these plans to judge whether your numbers hold together. A vague or overly optimistic plan is one of the fastest ways to stall your own application, so it pays to have this document prepared carefully rather than rushed together at the last minute.

5. Tax and duty incentives the certificate unlocks

This is usually the part that gets people most excited, and rightly so. Depending on your sector, a Certificate of Incentives can unlock exemptions on import duty for capital goods and machinery, deferment of value added tax on qualifying project equipment, and various corporate tax incentives tied to specific industries like agriculture, tourism, or manufacturing. These savings can be substantial for capital intensive projects, often outweighing the cost and effort of registration many times over within the first year or two of operation.

6. Land, work permits, and other practical unlocks

Beyond tax relief, the certificate solves two headaches that trip up many investors. Foreign investors cannot own land directly in Tanzania, but through this registration they can access derivative rights to land allocated by the government, which effectively gives long term, secure use of a site without breaching land ownership rules. The certificate also smooths the path to work permits and residence permits for foreign staff your project genuinely needs, something that can otherwise become a slow and frustrating process handled department by department.

7. The protection that matters for peace of mind

For any investor putting serious capital into a country, the biggest underlying worry is always whether that investment is safe from political or policy risk. The Tanzania Investment Act gives certificate holders protection against nationalisation and expropriation without fair compensation, along with clearer rights around repatriating profits and capital out of the country. This legal backing is often what convinces larger investors, both local and foreign, to commit fully rather than staying cautious.

8. What it costs and how long it takes

Compared to the value it unlocks, the certificate itself is not expensive. Expect to pay around eleven hundred US dollars in total, split between an application fee and the certificate issuance fee. Processing timelines vary depending on how complete your application is, but a well prepared submission with all documents in order typically moves through in a few weeks rather than months. Incomplete submissions, missing signatures, or vague feasibility studies are what stretch this timeline out unnecessarily.

9. Common reasons applications get delayed or rejected

Most delays trace back to the same handful of issues, capital evidence that does not clearly show the required threshold, a business plan that reads more like a pitch than a serious financial projection, land documentation that is incomplete or unclear, or a board resolution that was never properly signed and dated. None of these are complicated to fix, but they do require you to slow down and prepare rather than submit in a hurry hoping for the best.

10. Deciding whether your business should apply now or later

If your project is still finding its footing with modest capital, there is no rush and nothing lost by waiting. Registering too early, before you can genuinely demonstrate the required investment, just wastes time and fees on an application that cannot succeed yet. But if you are approaching or comfortably past the relevant threshold, whether as a Tanzanian entrepreneur scaling up or a foreign investor entering the market, this is exactly the moment to start preparing your documents. The incentives are real, the protections are real, and for the right size of investment, the certificate pays for itself many times over.
