Don't Bank Your Welfare on the Government: How to Protect Your Savings from Inflation and a Weakening Currency

No government can carry everyone's future. Inflation and a weaker shilling quietly eat the money you save. Here is why your welfare is your own job, and eight balanced ways to protect your savings.

Many of us grew up believing that someone else would take care of our future: the government would provide a job, a pension, free services and help in hard times. My message is simple: don't bank your welfare on the government. Governments do important work, but no government anywhere can carry the full weight of every citizen's future. If you want security in old age, in sickness or when the economy turns, you must build it yourself, and you must learn to protect your savings from the two silent thieves: inflation and a weakening currency.

Why You Cannot Leave Your Welfare to the Government

A government budget has to stretch across roads, schools, hospitals, security, water, electricity and debt repayments. Every year there is more demand than money. That means:

  • Public jobs are limited. Each year far more young people finish school and college than there are government vacancies.
  • Pensions are modest. A pension from NSSF or PSSSF is valuable, but for most people it replaces only part of their working income, and many self-employed people are not contributing at all.
  • Policies change. Taxes, fees, subsidies and rules can change from one budget to the next. A plan that depends entirely on a policy staying the same is fragile.
  • Hard times hit everyone at once. When prices rise or the economy slows, the government is under pressure too, exactly when you need help most.

This is not an attack on any government. It is simply realistic. Your welfare, and your family's, is first of all your responsibility.

The Silent Thieves: Inflation and a Weakening Shilling

Even people who save diligently can lose. Money kept under the mattress, or in an account that pays little or no interest, loses buying power every year. Headline inflation in Mainland Tanzania was 4.2% in October 2026, within the Bank of Tanzania's 3-5% target, with transport costs up about 14-15% (NBS). That sounds small, but it adds up:

Example: at about 4% inflation a year, TSh 1,000,000 kept in cash today will buy only what about TSh 676,000 buys today after 10 years. You have not "lost" a single note, yet a third of its value is gone.

The currency matters too, because so much of what we buy (fuel, medicines, machines, phones, imported stock) is priced in dollars. Look at how many shillings one US dollar has cost over the years:

The rate has been fairly steady in recent months, and the Bank of Tanzania works to keep it stable, but over the long run the trend has been downwards. When a government borrows heavily, or when too much money is created relative to what the economy produces, the result can be higher inflation and a weaker currency. Ordinary savers pay that price quietly.

How to Protect Your Savings: 8 Ways to Diversify

The answer is not to panic or to chase "get-rich-quick" schemes. It is diversification: spreading what you have across different kinds of assets, so that if one loses value, the others hold. Here are eight options, each with its benefits and its risks.

1. A business or side income

A business that sells goods or services people need tends to raise its prices along with inflation, so it protects you better than idle cash. If you are employed, start a side business in your free hours. Risk: many small businesses fail early, so test small before you invest big.

2. Skills

Skills are the asset no inflation can eat and no one can confiscate. A plumber, accountant, tailor, mechanic or digital marketer can earn in any economy. Risk: skills go out of date, so keep learning.

3. Land and property

Land and buildings can preserve value over the long term and can produce rent. But they do not "always skyrocket". Risks: property is illiquid (you cannot sell a plot in a day when you need cash), and land fraud is real: double sales, forged title deeds, and land without proper survey. Before you pay, do an official search of the title at the Ministry of Lands land registry, confirm the seller's identity, involve the local government office and use a lawyer.

4. Some foreign currency

Holding part of your savings in a strong foreign currency, for example a US dollar account at a licensed bank, is a lawful way to protect against shilling depreciation, especially if you import or plan to pay school fees or other costs abroad. Risks: exchange spreads and bank charges, and the shilling can also strengthen for a while (as it did in parts of 2025 and 2026), so keep it to a portion, not everything.

5. Gold

Gold has been a store of value for thousands of years and often holds up when currencies fall. Risks: the price swings sharply, physical gold must be stored safely, and fake or impure gold is common. Buy only from licensed dealers with proper receipts, and treat it as a small slice of your savings.

6. A pension: NSSF, PSSSF or a voluntary scheme

Make sure your contributions are actually being paid: check your member statement regularly. If you are self-employed, look into voluntary membership. Risk: a pension alone rarely covers all your needs, so treat it as one pillar, not the whole house.

7. Insurance

One hospital bill or one fire can wipe out years of savings. Health insurance (NHIF or a private scheme), and insurance for your shop, stock or vehicle, protects what you have built. Risk: read what is and is not covered before you sign.

8. Lower-risk interest-earning savings

For money you may need in the next few years, consider fixed deposits, unit trust funds (such as those run by UTT AMIS) or government Treasury bills and bonds, where yields have recently been above the inflation rate (the 364-day Treasury bill was about 6.3% in September 2026, per the Bank of Tanzania). Risk: returns can fall, and some products lock your money for a period.

⚠️ What about cryptocurrency? I do not recommend it as a way to protect savings. Prices swing wildly, scams and fake "investment platforms" are common, and the Bank of Tanzania has warned the public that cryptocurrencies are not legal tender in Tanzania. Be very careful with anyone who promises fast, guaranteed returns.

A Simple 5-Step Plan to Start This Month

  1. Build an emergency fund of 3 to 6 months of basic expenses in a safe, accessible account.
  2. Clear expensive debt first; high-interest loans grow faster than most savings.
  3. Check your pension and insurance: are contributions being paid, and are you covered for health?
  4. Start or grow an income source of your own, even a small one, and invest in one new skill.
  5. Diversify step by step. For example, Rehema, a teacher who saves TSh 300,000 a month, could put TSh 120,000 into a unit trust fund, TSh 90,000 towards a plot (after an official title search), TSh 60,000 into her tailoring side business, and TSh 30,000 into a US dollar account.

Note: this article is general education, not personal financial advice. Every person's situation is different; before making large decisions, talk to a licensed financial adviser, your bank or a lawyer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why shouldn't I depend on the government for my welfare?

Governments have limited budgets and many priorities, public jobs are few, pensions usually replace only part of your income, and policies can change. The government has a role, but your security in old age, sickness and hard times depends first on what you build yourself: income, skills, savings, a pension and insurance.

How can I protect my savings from inflation in Tanzania?

Do not keep large amounts idle in cash. Keep an emergency fund in a safe account, then spread the rest across options such as a business or side income, skills, unit trust funds or Treasury bills, land or property (after an official title search), and small portions of foreign currency or gold. Each has risks, so diversify rather than putting everything in one place.

Is buying land always a safe investment?

No. Land and property can preserve value over the long term, but they are hard to sell quickly and land fraud is common. Before paying, do an official search of the title at the Ministry of Lands land registry, verify the seller, involve the local government office and use a lawyer.

Should I buy cryptocurrency to protect my savings?

We do not recommend it. Cryptocurrency prices swing wildly, scams are common, and the Bank of Tanzania has warned that cryptocurrencies are not legal tender in Tanzania. Safer ways to protect savings include an emergency fund, unit trusts or government securities, a pension, insurance and building your own income.

Final Word

The government has a role to play, but your future is too important to leave entirely in anyone else's hands. Save, but do not let your savings sit idle while inflation and a weaker currency eat them. Build an income of your own, invest in your skills, protect yourself with a pension and insurance, and spread the rest across assets you understand. That is how you protect your savings and bank your welfare on the one person who will never forget you: yourself.
