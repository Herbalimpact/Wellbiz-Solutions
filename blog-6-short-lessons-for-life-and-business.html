6 Short Lessons for Life and Business: Risk, Solutions, Humility, Change, Relearning and the Courage to Try

Short sayings can carry big lessons. Here are six I have shared over the years, each with one practical step you can take this week.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

You do not always need a whole book to learn something important. Sometimes one sentence, read at the right moment, changes how you think. Over the years I have shared many short sayings on my Facebook page. Here I have collected six of them into a set of life and business lessons, and added to each one a practical step you can take this week.

1. The Poor Have Never Lost a Shilling; the Rich Have Lost Millions

"The world is full of poor people who have never lost a single shilling in their lives, and a few rich people, every one of whom has lost millions! Think about it..."

Ask any successful business owner about their journey and you will hear about losses: stock that did not sell, a partner who disappeared, a branch that had to close. They paid for their lessons. Many people who never move forward have one thing in common: they have never put anything at risk. They lose nothing, but they also gain nothing.

Experience does pay. A well-known Harvard study of start-ups backed by venture capital in the United States (Gompers and colleagues) found that first-time founders succeeded about 18% of the time, founders who had failed before about 20%, and founders who had succeeded before about 30%. Failure on its own is no guarantee; what counts is what you learn and whether you try again.

Practical step: set aside a small "learning budget", money you could lose without harming your family. Test your idea with that money first, and never borrow for an idea you have not tested.

2. Is Smartness Spending Energy on Blame, or Investing It in Solutions?

"Is being smart about wasting your energy pointing out mistakes, or about investing your energy in finding solutions?"

It is easy to blame: the government, the customers, the staff, the economy. Some of the criticism may even be fair. But blame does not pay the bills. Every successful business is, at heart, a solution to someone's problem: a shop close to home, transport that arrives on time, a service that saves people a trip. Every complaint you hear is a business opportunity waiting for someone who prefers solutions to blame.

Practical step: adopt the rule "one complaint, one proposal". This week, write down three problems your customers or neighbours complain about, and next to each one, a solution you could offer.

3. Everyone I Meet Is Better Than Me at Something

"In my walks, every man I meet is my superior in some way, and in that I learn of him." (Ralph Waldo Emerson, American essayist)

The security guard knows who comes and goes. The motorcycle-taxi rider knows the city's shortcuts and the prices in every market. Your most difficult customer knows exactly what your competitors do better. If you think you have nothing to learn from people "below" you, you close the door on free lessons every day. Humility is not weakness; it is a cheap way to grow.

Practical step: ask one person a day, "How do you do this so well?" and write down one thing you learned. In a year, that is 365 free lessons.

4. Poverty Persists Where People Are Not Ready to Change

"The only reason poverty continues to exist ... is the bad habit ... of not being ready to change." (_SK)

Let us be honest: poverty has many causes, including lack of capital, poor infrastructure and policies that make business hard. But among all those causes, there is one we control: our own readiness to change. The trader who refuses to keep records, the shop that will not accept mobile money, the farmer who plants the same way his grandfather did while the rains have changed: each is choosing the comfort of habit over growth.

Practical step: choose one habit to change for 30 days, for example writing down every sale, saving a fixed amount every day, or posting your products online twice a week. One small change a month is twelve changes a year.

5. Learn, Unlearn and Relearn

"The illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot learn, unlearn, and relearn."

This line is usually credited to the futurist Alvin Toffler. Strictly speaking, the idea belongs to the psychologist Herbert Gerjuoy, whom Toffler quoted in his 1970 book Future Shock; the wording we know today is a later paraphrase. Either way, the message is clear. As I once wrote: no matter how much classroom education you have, you need education outside the classroom all the time to keep up with change. When I attended a training on building the capacity of voluntary organisations in Putrajaya, Malaysia, I realised that however much you think you know, there are still many things you do not know, so learning must be a lifelong habit.

The evidence agrees: the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 found that employers expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030.

Practical step: name one belief to "unlearn" (for example, "a business must have a physical shop" or "marketing is only for big companies") and one new skill to learn this month.

6. The Only Barrier Between You and What You Want Is the Courage to Try

"The only barrier between a person and what they want in life is the heart to try..."

Most people do not fail; they simply never start. They wait for enough capital, the perfect time, or someone's permission. Yet trying is what opens doors: you learn what customers really want, you meet people who can help you, and you find out what works. Even a failed attempt teaches you more than a perfect plan that stays in your head. Jack Ma was rejected again and again before Alibaba; Colonel Sanders started KFC at 65.

Practical step: run a 30-day experiment. Write down what you will try, by when, and how you will measure success, for example "sell 20 units to neighbours before I rent a shop".

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most important life and business lessons?

There is no single list, but six lessons come up again and again: be willing to take calculated risks, spend your energy on solutions rather than blame, learn from everyone you meet, be ready to change your habits, keep learning, unlearning and relearning, and dare to try. Each one is simple; the hard part is practising it every week.

Is it true that every rich person has lost money?

Treat it as a lesson, not a statistic. Most people who build wealth through business have lost money on a deal, a product or a whole venture at some point, because anyone who invests faces losses. People who never risk a single shilling avoid losses, but they also avoid gains. The point is to take calculated, affordable risks and learn from them.

Who said "learn, unlearn and relearn"?

The popular line "the illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot learn, unlearn and relearn" is usually credited to Alvin Toffler. Strictly, the idea comes from the psychologist Herbert Gerjuoy, whom Toffler quoted in his 1970 book Future Shock; the familiar wording is a later paraphrase.

How can I try a business idea without risking my savings?

Start small and test before you invest: sell a few units to real customers, use only money you can afford to lose (a small "learning budget"), never borrow for an untested idea, and set a clear 30-day measure of success. If the test works, grow step by step; if it fails, the lesson costs you little.

Final Word

Six short sayings, six practical steps: dare to take calculated risks, look for solutions instead of blame, learn from everyone, be ready to change, keep learning, unlearning and relearning, and above all, have the heart to try. None of these lessons costs money. They only cost the decision to start.
