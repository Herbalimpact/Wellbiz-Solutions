ERB Registration for Engineers and Engineering Firms: A Step-by-Step Guide

A step-by-step breakdown of ERB registration categories for individual engineers and engineering firms, the documents required, and how to keep your registration in good standing.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Practising engineering in Tanzania without being registered with the Engineers Registration Board is not a grey area you can quietly work around. It is a legal requirement, and it applies whether you are a single graduate engineer looking to sign your first design, or a growing consulting firm bidding for infrastructure projects. The process itself is not complicated once you understand it, but the categories, documents, and steps involved trip up a surprising number of people simply because nobody laid them out clearly beforehand. Let us fix that here, step by step.

1. What ERB registration actually is and why it matters

ERB exists to keep a public register of everyone legally allowed to practise engineering in Tanzania, whether as an individual professional or as a firm offering consulting services. Registration is not a formality you can skip and catch up on later. Working on engineering projects without it, or signing off on designs you are not registered to certify, can expose you to penalties and disqualify your firm from tenders that specifically require registered practitioners. Clients, contractors, and regulators increasingly check registration status before engaging anyone, so this is one credential worth getting right from the start.

2. The registration categories for individual engineers

ERB does not treat every engineer the same way, and that is actually good news because it means there is a category that matches wherever you are in your career. Fresh diploma holders typically fall under Engineering Technician or Graduate Technician Engineer. Degree holders start as Graduate Engineers before progressing to Professional Engineer once they gain enough supervised experience and pass the required assessment. Engineers running their own consultancy practice move further still into Consulting Engineer or Independent Consulting Engineer status. Knowing which category actually fits your qualifications and experience level saves you from applying under the wrong one and having to start over.

3. Gathering your qualifications and documents first

Before you touch the online portal, put together your academic certificates, a clear record of your professional experience including employers, positions, durations, and projects you contributed to, and the contact details of at least two registered engineers willing to serve as references vouching for your competence. If you are aiming for Professional Engineer or Consulting Engineer status, you will also need evidence of the supervised experience years the category requires. Having all of this organised before you start the application saves you from the frustrating stop start experience of hunting for documents halfway through a form.

Wellbiz tip: Line up your certificates, employment records, and referee contacts before you start the ERB portal application — a category chosen without matching evidence is the single most common reason applications get rejected or asked to reapply.

4. Creating your ERB account and picking the right category

Registration happens through ERB's online platform, where you first create an account with your personal details, nationality, and contact information. Once your account is active, you select the registration category that matches your qualifications and experience, exactly as identified in the earlier step. Choosing carelessly here is one of the most common early mistakes, since applying under a category your qualifications do not yet support simply leads to rejection or a request to reapply correctly.

5. Submitting your application and paying the fee

With your category selected, you fill in the application form for that category and attach all your supporting documents in the format the portal requests, ideally clear scans rather than blurry phone photos. Once everything is uploaded, you pay the prescribed application fee for your category before submission is considered complete. Fees vary depending on whether you are registering as an individual engineer or applying for a more senior classification, so it helps to confirm the current fee for your specific category before you proceed.

6. What happens after you submit, as an individual

After submission, ERB reviews your qualifications, verifies your references, and checks that your supporting documents genuinely support the category you applied for. If anything is missing or unclear, expect a request for clarification rather than an outright rejection in most cases, so respond promptly if you are contacted. Once approved, you receive your registration certificate and an ERB registration number, which becomes the credential you will cite on every project, tender, and professional document going forward.

7. Why firm registration works differently

Registering a firm is a separate process from registering as an individual engineer, and it comes with its own conditions. A firm cannot register with ERB on the strength of its paperwork alone, it needs at least one partner or director who is already a registered Consulting Engineer or Independent Consulting Engineer, because ERB holds a person, not just a company, accountable for the technical work the firm produces. This is why many engineering consultancies wait until a founding partner has secured personal registration before applying for firm status.

8. Step by step: registering a local engineering consulting firm

To qualify as a Local Engineering Consulting Firm, at least fifty one percent of the firm's shares need to be held by consulting engineers who are Tanzanian citizens. From there, the firm gathers its certificate of incorporation from BRELA, a current business licence, tax clearance from the revenue authority, proof of office ownership or lease, documentary evidence of the equipment and facilities it owns, and a listing of the reference books, codes of practice, and design software it relies on. The firm also declares which of the eleven prescribed engineering disciplines it intends to practise in, since registration is discipline specific rather than a blanket approval to do any kind of engineering work.

9. What foreign engineering firms need to prepare instead

A firm with more than half its shares held by non-citizens falls under the Foreign Engineering Consulting Firm category instead, and the requirements shift accordingly. Alongside a Tanzanian certificate of incorporation or registration, foreign firms typically need to show a company profile of previous consulting work, current annual returns or founding documents if newly formed, evidence of an active contract or engagement in Tanzania, and increasingly, proof of professional indemnity insurance covering the project. The underlying principle stays the same though, ERB wants confidence that whoever is doing the technical work is qualified and accountable, regardless of where the firm originates.

10. Renewing your registration and staying compliant every year

Getting registered is not the finish line, it is the start of an annual routine. Both individual engineers and registered firms owe an annual fee due at the start of each calendar year, and lapsing on this can quietly put your registration status at risk right when you need it for a tender or project sign off. Increasingly, continuing professional development activity is tracked as part of maintaining your standing, so treat your registration as something to actively maintain rather than a certificate you file away and forget about once it arrives.
