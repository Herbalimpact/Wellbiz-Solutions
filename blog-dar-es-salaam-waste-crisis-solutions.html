Dirt Is Heating Up Dar es Salaam: Our Waste Problem, the Gases It Releases, and the Opportunity Hidden in It

Our city produces more waste than it can collect. As that waste rots in the streets it releases gases that trap heat. This is my opinion, my appeal to the government and NEMC, and the opportunities entrepreneurs can find inside the problem.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

The city of Dar es Salaam is dirty; there is no argument about that. It is very dirty because the amount of waste produced every day is large, while the capacity of the responsible authorities to collect it and take it to the dumpsite falls short. As a result, much of the waste stays in the streets, piling up and rotting in many small, informal dumps.

Dar es Salaam Waste: How Much Is Produced and How Much Is Collected?

In numbers: according to Dar es Salaam City Council statistics quoted by Health Policy Watch (October 2026), the city produces roughly 5,600 tonnes of waste every day, and less than 40% is formally collected. The main Pugu Kinyamwezi dumpsite, covering 65 hectares, receives about 2,500 tonnes a day, which is beyond its capacity.

That means more than half of the waste of a city of over 5.3 million people (2022 Census) never enters the formal system. It ends up in drains, on empty plots, along roadsides, or is burned at home. Even the official Pugu dump has its problems: in the dry season, gas from rotting food and plant waste sparks fires whose smoke troubles nearby residents.

The problem has not been ignored entirely. In September 2026 President Samia Suluhu Hassan described the state of cleanliness in Dar es Salaam as a disgrace and ordered a clean-up, and the authorities later announced fines of up to TSh 1,000,000 for littering. These are good steps, but fines alone do not collect rubbish. It is the system for collecting and processing waste that needs strengthening.

How Rotting Waste Adds to the Heat

Compressed rubbish (especially organic waste from food and plants) produces gases such as methane and carbon dioxide when it rots without enough air. These gases trap heat from the sun and hold it in the atmosphere, which raises air temperatures. They belong to the group known as greenhouse gases, which contribute heavily to harmful climate change.

  • Methane is powerful: over 20 years, methane traps more than 80 times as much heat as the same amount of carbon dioxide (UNEP / Climate and Clean Air Coalition).
  • Waste is a big source: about 20% of all human-caused methane worldwide comes from landfills and wastewater (UNEP, Global Methane Assessment 2021).

To be scientifically precise: methane from our waste adds to the warming of the whole planet, Dar es Salaam included. The day-to-day heat we feel in the streets comes more from the "urban heat island" effect: lots of concrete, asphalt and iron roofs, and too few trees. But waste burned in the streets adds smoke and polluted air that we breathe, so collecting waste helps both our health and the environment.

Our Appeal to the Government and NEMC

We ask the government to take firm action to make sure our environment is clean at all times. These steps should include:

  1. Equipping waste-collection contractors. The government should lend waste-collection contractors work equipment, including vehicles, according to need. Many small contractors fail to provide a reliable service simply because they do not have enough trucks.
  2. Finding an investor for a waste-processing plant. It is time for the government to get serious about finding an investor for a plant that processes waste into electricity and gas. Because such a plant needs raw material (which is rubbish), it will certainly take firm steps to make sure all the waste in the streets is collected and delivered to the plant.
  3. National Environment Management Council (NEMC), please help us with this. Enforcing environmental law, educating the public and monitoring dumpsites are an essential part of the solution.

Where things stand: according to the City Council's Waste Management Directorate, the government is seeking investors to upgrade the Pugu dump into a modern sanitary landfill able to generate energy from waste. Policy analysts say progress has been slowed by regulatory hurdles and delays in the public-private partnership (PPP) framework. That is exactly where we are asking for more speed.

What Can You and I Do Today?

  • ✅ Separate waste at home: food scraps on their own, plastics and cans on their own.
  • ✅ Pay your waste-collection fee and make sure your street's contractor comes on schedule.
  • ✅ Do not burn rubbish: the smoke is dangerous for your family's and neighbours' health.
  • ✅ Do not throw waste into drains: it causes flooding when the rains come.
  • ✅ Report informal dumps to your local government office or municipality.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much waste does Dar es Salaam produce per day?

According to Dar es Salaam City Council statistics quoted in October 2026, the city produces roughly 5,600 tonnes of waste a day, and less than 40% is formally collected. The main Pugu Kinyamwezi dumpsite receives about 2,500 tonnes a day.

Does rotting waste really make it hotter?

Yes, through greenhouse gases. When food and plant waste rots without enough air it releases methane, a gas that traps more than 80 times as much heat as carbon dioxide over 20 years, adding to global warming. Street-level heat in the city comes more from concrete, asphalt and a lack of trees, but burning waste adds to air pollution.

How would a waste-to-energy plant help?

A waste-processing plant needs rubbish as its raw material, so it has a business reason to make sure waste is collected from the streets and delivered to the plant. It can produce electricity or gas and reduce the methane escaping from dumpsites. The government is seeking investors to upgrade the Pugu dump for this purpose.

What businesses can I start from waste?

You can start a neighbourhood waste-collection service, buy and sort plastic bottles, cans and scrap metal, make compost, rear black soldier fly larvae for animal feed, or make fuel briquettes. Register your business and ask your municipal council and NEMC about permits before you start.

Final Word

Dar es Salaam belongs to all of us. Keeping it clean cannot be left to the government alone, nor to citizens alone. Let the government equip contractors and bring in a waste-processing plant; let NEMC enforce; and let us separate our waste, pay our fees and see the business opportunities inside our rubbish. A clean city is a healthier city, and one with more jobs.
