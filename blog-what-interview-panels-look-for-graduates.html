Good Grades Won't Get You the Job: What Interview Panels Really Look For (and What to Do If You've Been Searching for Over a Year)

Every candidate on the shortlist had excellent certificates. Only a few were hired. Here is what made the difference, and how to prepare for your next job interview.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Not long ago we were hired to give professional advice during the job interviews of several young university graduates who were competing for jobs at one institution.

All of these young people had certificates showing that they had passed their studies very well. If the jobs had been given on the basis of certificates alone, every one of them would honestly have deserved to be hired. Unfortunately, a graduation certificate was not the only important criterion in that interview. The other criteria the panel used made the job of filtering them easy, and at the end of the day only the few who were needed were found.

That Interview Opened Our Eyes

We realised that many young people enter the competition for jobs believing that their diplomas and degrees will carry them. They do not know what most employers are looking for, and so they do not bother to prepare.

Even so, most of these young people cannot be blamed. None of our big colleges and universities teaches the skill of handling a job interview. This means that a great many graduates go job hunting without being prepared. Many miss out on jobs not because they did badly in their studies, but because they lack the qualities the employer wants.

What Interview Panels Really Look For in a Job Interview

Grades tell the panel that you can learn. The interview is where they try to find out whether you can do the job, work with others and grow. These are the seven things most panels are quietly scoring:

What employers say: in the NACE Job Outlook 2025 survey, nearly 90% of employers said they look for evidence of problem-solving ability, nearly 80% look for teamwork, and at least 70% look for written communication, initiative, a strong work ethic and technical skills.

  1. Problem-solving. Can you think through a real situation? Expect questions like “What would you do if…?” or “Tell us about a difficult problem you solved.”
  2. Teamwork. Panels want to hear how you worked with others: a group project, a student association, a family business. What was your part, and how did you handle disagreement?
  3. Clear communication. How you answer matters as much as what you answer: short, organised, confident, in the language of the interview. Mumbling or memorised speeches lose marks.
  4. Initiative and work ethic. Have you ever started something without being told? Volunteered, organised, sold, taught? Employers want people who move without being pushed.
  5. Knowing the organisation and the role. A candidate who says “I will do any job” scores lower than one who explains why this job and this organisation, with facts about what it does.
  6. Evidence, not adjectives. “I am hardworking” is a claim. “During my field attachment I reorganised the store records and we found 40 missing items” is evidence.
  7. Attitude and integrity. Punctuality, respect, honesty about what you do not know, and a willingness to learn. Many panels will choose a teachable, reliable person over a brilliant but arrogant one.

How to Prepare: 6 Practical Steps Before Your Next Interview

  1. Read the advert line by line. Underline every duty and requirement. For each one, write one example from your life that shows you can do it.
  2. Build a “story bank” using STAR. Prepare five or six short stories: the Situation, your Task, the Action you took and the Result. Use them for teamwork, problem-solving, leadership, a mistake you learned from, and pressure.
  3. Prepare a 60-second introduction. “Tell us about yourself” is not a request for your life story. Say who you are, what you studied, one or two strengths with proof, and why you want this job.
  4. Practise out loud. Ask a friend to play the panel and ask you ten common questions. Record yourself on your phone and watch it. You will hear the “ehh…” and the rambling that you cannot hear in your head.
  5. Prepare two or three questions to ask. For example: “What would success look like in the first six months?” It shows you are thinking about the work, not only the salary.
  6. Handle the basics. Know the address, arrive 20 minutes early, dress one step smarter than the staff, carry copies of your documents, switch off your phone, and thank the panel at the end.

The Secret of Life: If You Have Been Searching for Over a Year

On another occasion I wrote a short post: if you are a graduate and you have spent more than a year going round looking for a job without success, know that the time has come to hang up your degree and start using your head. Know that in life nobody cares how many degrees you have, or how many “A”s you got in your school days. In life what is looked at is how many shillings you have. And know that, if you are not careful, your education can become an obstacle to your progress instead of the key to your life.

“Hanging up the degree” does not mean throwing it away. It means stopping waiting for the certificate to do the work for you, and starting to think and act. After a year, repeating the same method for another year is not a plan. First find out where you are getting stuck:

Frequently Asked Questions

What do interview panels look for besides good grades?

Most panels look for evidence that you can solve problems, work in a team, communicate clearly in speech and writing, take initiative and be reliable. In the NACE Job Outlook 2025 survey, nearly 90% of employers said they look for problem-solving ability and nearly 80% for teamwork. Your grades show you can learn; the interview is where you show you can do the job.

How should I prepare for a job interview as a fresh graduate?

Study the organisation and the job advert, prepare five or six short stories from your studies, projects, volunteering or part-time work using the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result), practise answering out loud with a friend, prepare two or three questions to ask the panel, and plan your outfit and route so you arrive early.

I have been looking for a job for over a year. What should I do?

Change the method, not just the number of applications. If you get no calls, fix your CV and cover letter. If you get interviews but no offers, work on your interview skills. Meanwhile gain experience through volunteering, an internship or part-time work, learn a skill employers are paying for, and consider earning some income on your own so that time does not pass empty.

Is it true that a degree is useless if you cannot find a job?

No. A degree proves you can learn difficult things and finish long work, and many employers still require one. But it is only a ticket into the competition. When everyone on the shortlist has a good degree, the job goes to the person who prepared best and showed the most practical value.

Final Word

To our graduates entering the job hunt for the first time: good luck. Your certificate has done its job by getting you to the door. Now prepare for the room. And if a year has passed, change the method, gain experience and start using your head, because the person who prepares wins more often than the person who only qualifies.
