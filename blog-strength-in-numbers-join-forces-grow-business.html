Strength in Numbers: Why Joining Forces Is the Small Trader's Best Weapon (and 5 Ways to Do It)

A bee is weak on its own, yet a swarm can make a whole street lock its doors. Small businesses work the same way.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

There is a lesson about business that most of us have watched in our own gardens. Strength in numbers is the only real weapon the weak have, and small businesses are, one by one, weak. Here is what the bee can teach us, what the arithmetic says, and five practical ways to join forces without losing ownership of your business.

The power of TSh 100: alone, one hundred shillings buys almost nothing today. But if 1,000,000 people each put TSh 100 into a common pot, the pot holds TSh 100 million in a single day.

Do You Know the Bee?

A bee is a very weak insect. On its own you can bully it however you like. It carries a single sting as its weapon, and once it has used it, that is the end.

Yet bees know their weakness, and so it is very rare to find them alone. Most of the time they move and work in huge colonies of hundreds of thousands, even millions. They understand that strength lies in numbers and unity. In their colonies, bees become fearsome opponents if provoked: a single swarm can make everyone on a street lock themselves indoors.

We humans should copy the bee. Combined strength is the one weapon that can give each of us a better and more secure life.

The Arithmetic of Strength in Numbers

Tanzania's 2022 Population and Housing Census counted about 61.7 million people, and the last national Household Budget Survey (2017/18) found that about 26% of people on the Mainland lived below the basic-needs poverty line. Among them are casual labourers, groundnut sellers, roadside water vendors and the people who collect used plastic bottles.

Even with all their poverty, most of them could put TSh 100 a day into a common basket without losing sleep. If just 7 million people did so, they would build a shared capital of TSh 700 million every day, or about TSh 255 billion a year.

In real life it is very hard to build a network that includes everyone. But networks of 10,000 to 100,000 members are possible. Picture 10,000 people in one ward who each contribute TSh 100 a day for their own development: TSh 1 million a day, TSh 365 million a year. With that capital every year, in ten years such a ward would see remarkable development, paid for with its own money. Can we organise ourselves?

How fast numbers grow: an illustration

Numbers also grow surprisingly fast. Suppose 100 founders each bring in two new members a day, and every new member does the same the next day. Membership doubles daily: 200, 400, 800, 1,600... By day 14 it passes 1.6 million, and by day 16 it passes 6.5 million. This is the mathematics of a geometric progression.

Warning: this doubling example only illustrates the power of numbers. Real groups grow through trust, not recruitment targets. Any scheme that pays you mainly for bringing in new members, rather than for selling real products, is a pyramid scheme: it collapses when recruitment slows and the last people in lose their money. Pyramid schemes are illegal in Tanzania.

Learning from Kampala: The Shared Shop

The idea of using strength in numbers to overcome individual weakness works in business too. Walk into a shop in Kampala or other Ugandan towns and it is common to find five or six young people all selling. Without asking, you might think they are employees and the owner is away. You would be wrong: the owners are the very people in front of you, and they have no boss but themselves.

These young traders understand that good business happens in recognised business districts, where rents are high and few of them could afford a shop alone. So five or six agree to rent one shop together. Each owns their own stock, and by special agreement they divide the product lines so they do not compete. With this simple arrangement they trade in prime business areas at a small cost each.

Why Going It Alone Keeps Many Young Traders Small

Many of our young traders work alone. When one builds a little capital, the shop usually goes to a back street, because there the trader can afford the rent alone and still have money left for stock. But these businesses often struggle to last: prices in back streets are low, and customers there do not buy in large amounts. The business survives, but it rarely grows.

Young traders need to change how they think. To trade in the city centre and compete for the best customers, they must build the habit of working together. We have a lot to learn from our neighbours.

5 Ways Small Businesses Can Join Forces

  1. Savings and loan groups (VICOBA/VSLA). 15 to 30 members buy shares every week, lend the pool to each other at an agreed charge and share out savings plus interest at the end of a 9 to 12-month cycle.
  2. The shared shop. 3 to 8 traders with different, non-competing products rent one shop in a busy location and split rent, security, power and the signboard.
  3. Group buying. Traders pool their orders, buy in bulk from the manufacturer or main wholesaler, share transport and negotiate a better price.
  4. Shared services. A delivery motorbike, a storeroom, a bookkeeper or a stand at a trade fair, shared by businesses that are not competitors.
  5. Cooperatives, SACCOS or a joint company. When the group is ready for bigger investments, formal registration brings legal protection and access to bank finance.

6 Steps to Start Joining Forces This Month

  1. Name the problem. Capital? Rent? Buying price? Choose the model that solves it.
  2. Choose partners for honesty, not friendship. Look for people who keep promises, pay on time and bring something the group lacks.
  3. Run a trust test. Work together on something small for one to three months, such as buying stock together once, before signing anything.
  4. Write the rules. A constitution or partnership agreement covering contributions, leadership, loans, costs, leaving and disputes, signed by everyone.
  5. Make money transparent. Count money in front of everyone, never mix it with personal money, and use a three-key box or an account needing two or more signatures.
  6. Register. Community savings groups such as VICOBA must register with their district or municipal council under the Microfinance Act, 2018; a shared shop needs the right business registration, TIN and licence. Ask your council and TRA what applies to you.

A worked example: six traders, one shop

Six young traders each paid about TSh 150,000 a month for a small back-street shop and sold about TSh 250,000 a day at a 20% margin. Together they rented a larger shop on a busy road for TSh 1.2 million a month, each selling a different line, and each trader's sales rose to about TSh 400,000 a day.

Tip: choose partners whose products complement yours. A customer who comes in for a phone may leave with a shirt and a pair of shoes, and every partner wins.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "strength in numbers" mean in business?

It means that small businesses which pool their money, space or buying power can do together what none can do alone: rent a shop in a busy location, buy stock in bulk at a lower price, or lend each other capital through a savings group, while each keeps ownership of their own business.

How do I start a savings group (VICOBA)?

Gather 15 to 30 people who know each other, agree a written constitution (share value, loan rules, interest, fines, cycle length), elect leaders, keep money in a three-key box or an account needing two or more signatures, meet regularly and register the group with your district or municipal council.

How does a shared shop work?

Three to eight traders selling different, non-competing products rent one shop together. Each owns their own stock and keeps their own records, while rent, security, power and the signboard are split by the space each uses, under a signed partnership or sub-letting agreement.

Is a scheme that pays me for recruiting members a good way to use strength in numbers?

No. A scheme that pays mainly for bringing in new members rather than for selling real products is a pyramid scheme. It collapses when recruitment slows and the last people in lose their money. Pyramid schemes are illegal in Tanzania.

Final Word

Remember the bee. Alone, it is easy to crush. Together, bees build what no single bee ever could. Strength in numbers is not about giving up your own business; it is about sharing the costs and risks that are too heavy to carry alone. Start small, choose honest partners, write everything down, and let the numbers work for you.
