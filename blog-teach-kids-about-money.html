How to Teach Kids About Money: A Parent's Guide by Age (5-17)

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Think back to your own school days. You probably learned algebra, the rivers of a faraway continent and the dates of old wars. Did anyone teach you how to make a budget, why a "small" mobile loan can cost more than a bank loan, or how to spot a pyramid scheme?

For most of us across Africa, Asia and Latin America, the answer is no. Few school systems teach financial literacy, and many families never talk about money at all. So each generation learns the hard way, through debt, scams and salaries that vanish before month-end. Then it passes the same habits on to the next one.

Waiting for governments or schools to change the curriculum could take decades. Your child can't wait that long. The good news is that parents can teach financial literacy at home, starting today, with coins, three jars and 15 minutes a week. This guide shows you how, age by age.

Why parents, not schools, must lead

Research carried out by the University of Cambridge for the UK's Money Advice Service found that many of our basic money habits are formed by about age seven. Children learn them at home, mostly by watching how the adults around them earn, spend, save and talk about money.

That means you are already your child's money teacher, whether you plan to be or not. The only question is whether you teach on purpose. And it is a powerful lever: a child who learns to save before spending and to avoid debt traps can break a cycle that has held a family back for generations, regardless of how much money the family has today.

Three promises before you start

  1. Talk about money openly, without shame or fear. Money is a tool, not a secret.
  2. Let children make small mistakes now. Losing 100 at age nine is far cheaper than losing a million at age thirty.
  3. Model what you teach. If you ask them to save, let them see you save too, even a little.

You don't need to be good with money yourself. Many parents find that teaching their children fixes their own habits. Learn together.

Ages 5-8: Little Savers

At this age, keep everything physical and visual. Children need to see and touch money.

  • What money is and where it comes from. Explain that money comes from work, not from the cash machine or the phone. Take a "job walk" through the market and ask: "What is this person selling? Who pays them?"
  • Needs vs wants. Food, water, school and medicine are needs. Sweets and toys are wants. When shopping together, let your child label each item in the basket.
  • The three jars: Save, Spend, Share. Every time your child receives money, they split it. A simple rule: for every 10 coins, 4 to Save, 5 to Spend, 1 to Share. This single habit is the foundation of everything else.
  • Earning. Separate unpaid family jobs (making the bed) from small earning jobs (watering plants, sorting beans). Children learn responsibility and that work creates income.
  • Waiting. Offer one sweet now or three on Saturday. Children who learn to wait become adults who can save.

Ages 9-12: Smart Spenders

Now your child is ready to manage real money, with real (small) consequences.

  • A regular allowance. Give a small, predictable amount and agree in writing what it must cover, such as school snacks or airtime. The hardest rule is for you: don't top it up when it runs out. One hungry afternoon teaches more than a lecture.
  • A simple budget. Savings first, then needs, then wants. Compare the plan with reality at the end of each week.
  • A savings goal with a price and a date. Price divided by weeks equals the amount to save weekly. Consider a "parent match" (you add 1 for every 2 they save) to show the power of saving early.
  • Advertising tricks. Watch adverts together and spot the tricks: celebrities, "only today", 999 instead of 1,000. Introduce the 48-hour rule for bigger wants.
  • Borrowing costs extra. If your child asks for an advance, lend it at "interest": borrow 100, repay 110. Most children quickly decide borrowing isn't fun. That's the lesson.
  • Banks and mobile money. Show how a balance and a transaction fee work, and drill one rule: never share a PIN, with anyone, ever.

Ages 13-17: Future Builders

Teenagers learn best through honest discussion, not lectures. Ask more questions than you answer, and share your real money decisions, including your mistakes.

  • Where adult money really goes. Let them guess your household's monthly costs, then show the real numbers. Explain a payslip: gross pay minus tax and deductions equals take-home pay.
  • The 50/30/20 budget. 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings. On a low income, protect at least 10% savings and cut wants first.
  • Compound growth. Saving 10,000 a month at 10% a year grows to about 2 million after 10 years, and about 22.6 million after 30 years, from 3.6 million actually saved. (Illustration only; real returns vary.) Starting at 18 instead of 35 changes everything.
  • Debt traps. A 10,000 phone loan with a 10% monthly fee, repeated every month, costs 12,000 a year in fees, more than the loan itself. Teach the rule: never borrow for consumption or to repay another loan.
  • Scams and "get rich quick" schemes. Pyramid schemes, fake forex and crypto platforms, "double your money" groups and fake job fees target young people on social media every day. Teach the warning signs: guaranteed high returns, rewards for recruiting, pressure to decide fast, and requests for PINs or upfront fees.
  • Saving groups. Whether you call it a chama, susu, esusu, stokvel, tontine or tanda, rotating savings groups are a powerful tool across the developing world. Teach both their strengths and their risks.
  • Assets vs liabilities. Before any big purchase, ask: will this put money in my pocket or take money out?
  • A first income project. Let your teen run a small business for three months, such as baking, tutoring, poultry or social media help for local shops, keeping simple records of money in and money out.

Make it a habit: the monthly family money meeting

Once a month, sit together for 30 minutes, phones away. Share one good money decision each, check the jars and goals, make one real family decision together ("We have 50,000 left. Save it for school fees, fix the gate or have an outing?"), then choose the next lesson. Children who are included in real decisions grow up confident with money.

Frequently asked questions

At what age should I start teaching my child about money?

Around age 5, or as soon as your child can count coins. Many basic money habits are formed by about age 7, so early, simple lessons matter most. It is never too late, though: teenagers can start at the tier above their level and move quickly.

How much pocket money should I give my child?

There is no correct amount. Choose a small, regular amount you can afford every time, agree what it must cover, and don't top it up when it runs out early. The habit matters more than the size.

Should I pay my children for doing chores?

Use two lists: family jobs everyone does for free, and extra earning jobs that can be paid. This teaches both responsibility and that work creates income.

Can I teach my child about money if I'm not good with money myself?

Yes. Learn alongside your child, be honest about your own mistakes, and follow a structured guide. Your child doesn't need a perfect parent, just a willing one.
