Why Most Businesses Die Before Their 5th Birthday (and How a Business Plan Keeps Yours Alive)

About half of all new businesses close before they turn five. Here is why it happens, and the simple step that protects your savings and your business.

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Every year thousands of people open a shop, a salon, a food stall, a hardware store or a small agency with high hopes. A few years later, many of those doors are closed. The owners have lost their savings, sometimes their family's savings too, and often they never find out exactly what went wrong.

Why does this happen so often? And, more importantly, what can you do so that your business is still standing on its fifth birthday?

How Many Businesses Really Die Early?

The most reliable long-term figures come from the United States Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), which tracks every new business establishment year after year. Its data shows a clear and worrying pattern:

Age of the businessShare still operating
After 1 yearAbout 80% (1 in 5 has already closed)
After 5 yearsAbout 50% (half have closed)
After 10 yearsAbout 35% (nearly two in three have closed)

That is in one of the easiest countries in the world to borrow money, hire skilled staff and find customers. In Tanzania and much of East Africa, where small businesses face higher interest rates, tougher access to credit and a large informal sector, the risk of an early death is, if anything, greater.

The bottom line: roughly half of all new businesses will not reach their fifth birthday. The question is not whether the risk is real, but which side of that line your business will be on.

The 4 Main Reasons Young Businesses Die

Research and my own experience advising small business owners in Dar es Salaam point to the same handful of causes.

1. Too little capital

Many businesses start with just enough money to open the door, and nothing left to keep it open. Rent, stock, licences and salaries must be paid long before customers arrive in good numbers. When the cash runs out before sales pick up, the business dies, even if the idea was good. Running out of cash is consistently one of the top reasons start-ups fail worldwide.

2. Not being able to hire the right people

An owner who has to do everything alone soon hits a wall. Without the people needed to sell, serve, keep the books or manage stock, quality drops, customers leave and the owner burns out. Many small businesses never plan for the staff they will need, or for how they will pay them.

3. The owner's knowledge and skills

Being good at a trade is not the same as being good at running a business. A talented tailor, mechanic or cook may know little about pricing, record keeping, marketing or managing cash. Those gaps are rarely visible on day one, but they show up within the first two or three years.

4. The wrong reasons for starting

Some people start a business because a friend is doing well in the same line, because they lost a job and need something quickly, or because they have a lump sum and feel they must "do something" with it. A business started without a clear understanding of its customers, its competition and its numbers is a business started on hope.

The Common Thread: No Business Plan

Look closely and all four reasons share one root. Most businesses die early because they were started without a proper business plan.

A business plan is to a business what an architect's drawing is to a house. You would never build a house without a plan: the walls would be in the wrong place, the roof might not fit and the whole structure could collapse after the first heavy rains. A business started without a plan has exactly the same weaknesses. It may stand for a while, but it is unlikely to last.

A good business plan forces you to answer the hard questions before you spend your money, not after.

7 Questions Your Business Plan Must Answer

  1. Who exactly are my customers, and why will they buy from me instead of the shop next door?
  2. How much money do I really need to open, and to keep running for at least six months with low sales?
  3. When will I break even? How many sales a day or a month do I need to cover all my costs?
  4. What will I charge, and does that price leave a real profit after every cost is counted?
  5. Who will I need to hire, when, and how will I pay them?
  6. What skills am I missing, and how will I learn them or bring them in?
  7. What will I do if things go wrong? A slow season, a new competitor, a price rise from suppliers.

If you cannot answer these questions on paper, you are not ready to invest. If you can, you have already removed most of the reasons businesses die young. You also have the document banks, investors and partners will ask to see when you need more capital.

Already Running a Business? Watch for These Warning Signs

  • You don't know, without guessing, whether you made a profit last month.
  • Business money and household money come from the same pocket.
  • You often borrow to restock or to pay rent.
  • Sales depend on you being in the shop every single day.
  • You have no written plan for the next 12 months.

If two or more of these sound familiar, it is not too late. Writing a business plan now, even for a business that is already running, is one of the cheapest ways to protect what you have built.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of businesses fail within 5 years?

According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, about 20% of new businesses close in their first year and about half close within five years. Roughly two in three are gone after ten years.

What is the main reason small businesses fail?

The most common causes are too little capital, being unable to hire the right people, gaps in the owner's business skills and starting for the wrong reasons. Most of these come from starting without a proper business plan.

Does a business plan really help a business survive?

Yes. A business plan makes you work out your customers, costs, break-even point, staffing and risks before you invest, which removes most of the reasons young businesses fail. It is also what banks and investors ask for.

Is it too late to write a business plan if my business is already running?

No. A plan written for a running business helps you see where money is being lost, set targets for the next 12 months and prepare for a loan or new investment.

Final Word

Half of all new businesses close before they turn five. Most of them don't fail because the owner was lazy or the idea was bad. They fail because they were built without a plan. Build yours on paper first, and give your business the best chance of celebrating many birthdays.
