RITA Registration Explained: When You Need It Beyond BRELA

Trusts, NGOs, and business names get mixed up constantly in Tanzania — and the mix-up usually involves RITA. Here's what RITA actually registers, what it doesn't, and exactly which office to walk into for each one.

Ask ten people in Dar es Salaam what RITA does, and you'll get ten different answers — most of them wrong. Some think it registers NGOs. Others assume it's where you reserve a business name. A few confuse it entirely with BRELA. The truth is narrower and more specific than any of that, and getting it wrong wastes weeks: you queue at the wrong office, submit the wrong form, or pay a fee to an agency that was never going to issue you the certificate you needed. Here's what the Registration Insolvency and Trusteeship Agency actually does, where it fits next to BRELA and the NGO Coordination Board, and how to tell — in under a minute — which registry your organization actually belongs at.

1. What RITA actually is, and why the confusion exists

RITA — the Registration Insolvency and Trusteeship Agency — was launched on 23 June 2006 as an executive agency under the Ministry of Justice and Constitutional Affairs. Its confusion with BRELA is historical: before a 1967 reorganization, the registrar that became RITA also handled business names and other commercial registrations. Those functions moved elsewhere decades ago and now sit with BRELA, but the association never fully died out in public memory. Today RITA's mandate covers four distinct areas: registration of vital events (births, deaths, marriages, divorces, and adoptions), Trusteeship under the Trustees' Incorporation Act, the office of the Public Trustee handling probate and estate administration, and insolvency matters such as bankruptcy and company liquidation. Notably, business names are not on that list — a point worth remembering before you read any further.

2. Trusts: the one place RITA genuinely overlaps with "starting an organization"

This is where RITA becomes directly relevant to founders. If you're forming a body for a religious, educational, literary, scientific, social, or charitable purpose — a community welfare group, a religious congregation's governing board, a family foundation, a professional or sports association — you likely need Trustees' Incorporation under the Trustees' Incorporation Act, Cap 318, and that process runs through RITA, not BRELA. Incorporation gives your trustees' board its own corporate personality, perpetual succession regardless of who currently sits on the board, and the legal capacity to hold land and enter contracts in the organization's name rather than in individual trustees' personal names. As of late 2025, RITA had registered 5,211 Trustees' Boards nationally — 2,876 community-based, 2,161 religious, 150 family trusts, plus smaller numbers of political-party and sports bodies — and the agency has been actively digitizing the process through its "eRITA" platform.

3. How Trustees' Incorporation actually works

The process centers on Form TI.1. You'll need your organization's constitution setting out its objectives and governance structure, identification and CVs for the proposed trustees, and — this is the step most applicants underestimate — a recommendation letter from the relevant local government authority confirming the group is known and legitimate in its area of operation. The statutory fee is TZS 200,000. Once RITA is satisfied the application meets the Act's requirements, the certificate of incorporation is issued by the Administrator General, and from that date the trustees' board can operate, hold assets, and sign agreements as a single legal entity rather than as a collection of individuals each personally exposed. Skipping this step is the reason many long-running community groups still can't open an account or own the land they've operated from for years — because everything is technically titled to individuals who may no longer even be involved.

Wellbiz tip: If your trustees' board has changed membership since incorporation and you never notified RITA, treat that as unfinished business, not a technicality. An outdated trustee record is exactly the kind of gap that surfaces at the worst possible moment — when a bank account needs updating or a piece of land needs to change hands.

4. Where NGOs actually fit in — and it isn't RITA

Here's the mix-up that trips up the most people: NGOs in Tanzania are not registered by RITA. They fall under the NGO Act No. 24 of 2002, as amended in 2019, and are registered by the Registrar of NGOs under the NGO Coordination Board — a completely separate body from RITA, with its own office, its own forms, and its own fee schedule. Registration is tiered by the geographic scope of your operations: roughly TZS 80,000 for a District-level NGO, TZS 100,000 for Regional, TZS 115,000 for National, and USD 350 for an International NGO operating in Tanzania. If your organization's founding documents describe it as an NGO carrying out development, advocacy, or humanitarian work rather than a religious, educational, or purely charitable trust, the NGO Coordination Board — not RITA — is where your application belongs. Some organizations genuinely qualify for either Trustees' Incorporation or NGO registration depending on how they're structured; if you're unsure which category actually fits your objectives, that's a conversation worth having before you file anything, not after.

5. Business names: the mix-up that costs people the most time

This is the myth that needs killing outright: registering a business name in Tanzania has nothing to do with RITA. Business names are governed by the Business Names (Registration) Act, Cap 213, and registered through BRELA — the Business Registration and Licensing Agency — the same office that issues company certificates of incorporation. If you're a sole trader or a small partnership operating under a trade name rather than forming a limited company, BRELA's business name registration is the correct — and only — route. Anyone who tells you to take a business name application to RITA is repeating outdated information from before the 1967 reorganization, and following that advice will cost you a wasted trip and nothing else.

6. RITA's other roles you're less likely to need, but should know about

Beyond trusteeship, RITA runs civil registration of births, deaths, marriages, divorces, and adoptions nationwide — the vital events records that underpin everything from passport applications to inheritance claims. Its Office of the Public Trustee handles probate and administration of deceased estates where no willing or capable administrator exists within the family. And its insolvency function covers bankruptcy proceedings for individuals and liquidation or receivership for companies that can no longer meet their obligations. None of these apply to most people setting up a new organization, but they explain why RITA's full name includes "Insolvency" alongside "Trusteeship" — it's a broader agency than its trust-registration role alone suggests, and the same office that helps you incorporate a trustees' board is also the office that would, in a very different scenario, wind one down.

7. The one-minute decision framework

Before you file anything, ask what you're actually forming. A limited company, or a sole trader or partnership trading under a business name — that's BRELA. A body organized for religious, educational, literary, scientific, social, or charitable purposes that wants its trustees' board to hold property and operate as one legal entity — that's RITA, under the Trustees' Incorporation Act. A development, advocacy, or humanitarian organization structured as an NGO — that's the NGO Coordination Board, not RITA. And if you need a birth, death, or marriage certificate, or you're dealing with a deceased relative's estate or a company insolvency — that's also RITA, just a different department entirely. Three agencies, three very different purposes, and picking correctly the first time is the difference between a straightforward application and weeks of resubmitted paperwork.
