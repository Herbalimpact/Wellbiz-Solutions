Jobs Are Few — But Even Among the Few, Where Are You Falling Short?

Why a degree alone isn't enough, and the preparation that separates those who get hired from those who don't

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Every year, thousands of young people graduate from our colleges and universities full of hope. A few months later, many of them reach the same conclusion: "There are no jobs."

It's true that the job market is tough. There are fewer openings than graduates. But there's a question few people stop to ask: the handful of vacancies advertised every day — who is getting them, and why isn't it me?

More often than not, the answer isn't in the market. It's in the preparation.

A Degree Gets You to the Door — It Doesn't Get You the Job

We were raised to believe that a good degree — an Upper Second, or even a First Class — means the job will come looking for you. Some employers do filter applicants by degree class alone, and for them, your grade carries real weight.

But most employers don't stop there. They want to assess you as a real person: how you express yourself, how you think, how confident you are, and how well you'll fit into their team. In that process, your certificate is only your ticket in. What decides the outcome is you.

That's why you'll see someone with an ordinary degree get hired while someone with a top degree misses out. The difference wasn't intelligence — it was preparation.

The hiring process has two big stages, and many job seekers fail at each one.

Stage One: Your Cover Letter and CV — The First Door

Many graduates don't realise that writing a job application letter is a skill, not a form-filling exercise. A single employer may receive hundreds of letters for one position and skim through them quickly. A letter that doesn't grab attention within seconds gets set aside — and your chance ends there, before you've even been seen.

What a winning cover letter looks like

  • It is written for that specific job, not one copy sent everywhere.
  • It shows what you can do for the employer, not just what you need.
  • It is short, clean, and free of grammar and spelling mistakes.
  • It highlights skills and experience that match the advertised requirements directly.

Your letter doesn't travel alone. It goes with your CV (or résumé). The two work as a pair: the letter draws the employer in, and the CV proves your case. A poorly organised CV, one that's far too long, or one that hides your real achievements can undo all the good work your letter did.

Stage Two: The Job Interview — Where Many Lose the Chance They Already Had

You've been invited to an interview. Congratulations — you've passed a stage most applicants never pass. But this is exactly where many make their biggest mistake: they walk in unprepared, assuming they'll simply answer whatever they're asked.

Interviewing is a skill too. The people who succeed aren't necessarily the best candidates — often they're the best prepared, and they manage to impress their interviewers. Here's how to prepare for a job interview in five areas:

1. Know your employer

What is the company's or institution's history? What does it do? Where are its strengths, and what are its challenges? When you walk in with that understanding, you can present yourself as a solution to their challenges — not just someone looking for a salary.

2. Understand the role you're applying for

What kind of person does the job need? Calm and detail-oriented? Energetic and confident in front of people? Reflect honestly: which of your qualities match the role, and be ready to explain them with real examples.

3. Plan what you'll wear

Your appearance speaks before you open your mouth. Dress in a way that suits the work environment — clean, neat and respectful.

4. Walk in with confidence

Go in believing you can do the job. Confidence doesn't mean boasting; it means speaking calmly, making eye contact with the interviewer, and answering clearly.

5. A question you can't answer isn't the end of the road

If you're asked something you don't know, don't panic and don't make up an answer. Be honest, then show that you're someone who learns quickly — and if possible, explain how you would go about finding the solution. Employers are often more impressed by someone who can learn than by someone pretending to know everything. (It's one reason emotional intelligence often beats credentials in hiring.)

Conclusion: Change the Question You're Asking

Young person, instead of asking yourself "Why are there no jobs?", start asking:

  • Does my application letter truly capture an employer's attention?
  • Does my CV show my real value?
  • How well have I prepared for my next interview?

The job market won't change tomorrow. But your preparation can change today. And in a market with few openings, the best-prepared candidate is the one who takes that single spot.

Your degree got you to the door. Your preparation is what will get you inside.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't graduates find jobs even with a good degree?

A degree gets you to the employer's door, but most employers also assess how you express yourself, how you think, your confidence and how you'll fit the team. Candidates with ordinary grades often get hired over those with top grades because they prepared better.

What makes a good job application cover letter?

It's written for that specific job, shows what you can do for the employer, is short and error-free, and highlights skills that match the advertised requirements.

How do I prepare for a job interview?

Research the employer, understand the role and prepare real examples, dress neatly for the environment, go in confident but not boastful, and answer honestly when you don't know something.

What should I do if I don't know the answer to an interview question?

Don't panic or invent an answer. Be honest, show you learn quickly, and explain how you would find the solution.
