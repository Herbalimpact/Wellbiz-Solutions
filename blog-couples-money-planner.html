Money and Marriage: How Couples Can Plan Money Together (and Fight Less)

🇹🇿 Soma kwa Kiswahili

Does this sound familiar? Payday comes. One of you wants to save, the other wants to fix the car. A relative calls asking for help with school fees. By the end of the month, nobody is quite sure where the money went — and somehow, it turns into an argument.

You're not alone. Money is one of the most common reasons couples argue. But here's the good news: it's rarely about how much money you have. It's about not having a plan you both agreed on. Once you have that plan, money stops being a fight and starts being a team project.

Here are seven simple steps to get there.

1. Start by sharing your money stories

We all learned about money as children. Maybe you grew up where every coin was counted. Maybe money was never talked about at all. These early lessons shape how we spend and save today — and they explain a lot of "Why does my partner do that?!" moments.

Sit down together with a cup of tea and take turns answering:

  • How was money handled in your home when you were growing up?
  • What worries you most about money?
  • Are you more of a saver or a spender?
  • What does "being financially secure" mean to you?

Just listen. No judging, no fixing. You'll probably learn something new about each other.

2. Decide how you'll combine your money

There's no single "right" way. Most couples choose one of these:

  • All together: everything goes into one pot.
  • Separate: each of you keeps your own money and pays agreed bills.
  • Yours, mine and ours: you each put an agreed amount into a shared pot for the household, and keep the rest for yourselves.

For many couples, "yours, mine and ours" is the sweet spot. You work as a team on the big things, and each of you still has some freedom.

3. Split costs in a way that feels fair

Splitting everything 50/50 sounds fair — until one of you earns a lot more than the other. A simple alternative is to split by income: you each pay the same percentage of what you earn.

Here's an example. Daniel earns 2,000 a month and Grace earns 1,600. Their household costs are 3,330.

4. Build one shared budget (and keep some "no questions asked" money)

List your household costs together: rent, food, electricity and water, transport, school fees, health, savings and so on. Give every shilling or dollar a job until nothing is left unplanned.

Then here's the secret ingredient: give each of you some personal spending money, no questions asked. It might be for a haircut, lunch with friends or a small treat. It keeps the peace — nobody has to explain every small purchase.

When one of you pays a shared bill from your own pocket, write it down. At the end of the month, add it up and settle who owes whom. (Our planner does this for you automatically.)

5. Agree on how much to give to family

Helping parents, brothers and sisters, and contributing to weddings and funerals is a big part of life for many of us. It's a blessing — but it can quietly drain a household, especially when one partner gives without the other knowing.

The fix is simple: agree on a monthly limit for each side of the family, plus one for community contributions, before the requests come in. When a request is bigger than the limit, talk first. "Let me check with my partner" is a complete, respectful answer.

6. Put every debt on the table

Debt isn't shameful. Hidden debt is what hurts trust. List every loan together — bank loans, mobile loans, SACCOS, credit cards, money borrowed from friends — with the balance and monthly payment.

Then decide together: Are debts from before you met shared or personal? Which one will you pay off first? And what's your rule for new borrowing? (A good one: "No new loan unless we both agree.")

7. Dream together — and meet once a month

What do you want to build? A plot of land, your own home, a business, your children's education, a family holiday? Write your goals down, put a price and a date on each, and work out how much to save every month. Start with an emergency fund — it protects every other goal.

Finally, have a monthly money date. Thirty minutes, something nice to drink, and this simple agenda:

  1. Celebrate one money win from the month.
  2. Check your budget: planned vs actual.
  3. Settle up — who owes whom?
  4. Review family support, debts and goals.
  5. Plan next month, and say one thing you appreciate about each other.

Frequently asked questions

Should married couples have a joint account?

Many couples do well with "yours, mine and ours": a joint account for household costs and shared goals, plus a personal account each. You get teamwork and freedom at the same time.

How should we split bills when one of us earns more?

Try splitting by income. You each pay the same percentage of what you earn, so the one who earns more pays more — and both of you keep a fair share for yourselves.

How do we handle money requests from family?

Agree on a monthly limit for each side of the family before requests come in. For anything bigger, talk first and decide together.

How often should couples talk about money?

Once a month is a great rhythm. A 30-minute money date keeps small issues from turning into big fights.
